Phillips-Curve Expectations
THE CORE IDEA
Expected inflation and supply shocks shift the short-run Phillips curve rather than moving the economy along
one curve. Higher expected inflation raises actual inflation at each unemployment rate and shifts the curve
upward; lower expectations shift it downward. Adverse supply shocks can also raise inflation at a given
unemployment rate. A demand change, by contrast, moves the economy along the current short-run curve
while expectations remain fixed.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
A movement along one short-run Phillips curve reflects a change in aggregate demand with expected inflation
fixed.
•
Higher expected inflation shifts the short-run Phillips curve upward; lower expected inflation shifts it downward.
•
An adverse supply shock can shift the curve upward by raising inflation at each unemployment rate.
•
Compare inflation at the same unemployment rate to identify a shift rather than a movement along the curve.
WATCH OUT
Do not confuse a curve shift with movement along one curve. At the same unemployment rate, a higher
SRPC reflects higher expected inflation or a worse tradeoff.
WORKED EXAMPLE: SHIFTING THE PHILLIPS CURVE
The economy begins at point b on SRPC2 with
unemployment U2 and inflation I2. When expected
inflation falls, the short-run Phillips curve shifts
downward to SRPC1. At the natural unemployment
rate U2, the economy reaches point c with lower
inflation I1. Expectations, not a permanent
unemployment change, drive the shift.
