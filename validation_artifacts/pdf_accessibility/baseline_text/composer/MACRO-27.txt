Inflation Tax and Deflation
THE CORE IDEA
Money creation can finance government spending; the revenue from creating money is seigniorage. When
prices rise, existing money balances lose purchasing power, creating an inflation tax on money holders.
Disinflation means slower inflation; deflation means a falling price level. Deflation raises the purchasing
power of cash but also raises the real burden of a fixed nominal debt.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Money-financed government spending can erode the purchasing power of existing cash balances.
•
A fall from 6% to 2% inflation is disinflation: prices still rise.
•
Negative inflation means deflation: the price level falls.
•
With a fixed nominal debt, lower prices raise the amount of goods needed to repay it.
WATCH OUT
Do not assume a tax must always be explicit and legislative. Inflation acts like a tax on money holders
by reducing the purchasing power of their cash balances.
WORKED EXAMPLE: PURCHASING POWER OF CASH
A household holds $1,000 in cash while the price level rises 10%. Its purchasing power becomes
$1,000 / 1.10 = $909.09 in initial-price dollars, a 9.09% loss. If the price level instead falls 10%, a fixed
$1,000 debt has a real repayment burden of $1,000 / 0.90 = $1,111.11 in initial-price dollars.
