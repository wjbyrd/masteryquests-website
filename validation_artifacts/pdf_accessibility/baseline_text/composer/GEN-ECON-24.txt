International Trade: Tariffs
THE CORE IDEA
A tariff is a tax on imported goods that raises the domestic price above the world price. Domestic firms
respond by producing more, consumers buy less, and imports fall. Consumers lose surplus, domestic
producers gain surplus, and the government collects tariff revenue. The tariff also prevents mutually
beneficial trades, creating deadweight loss from excessive domestic production and reduced consumption.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Begin with an importing country whose world price is below the domestic no-trade price.
•
Add the tariff per unit to the world price to find the tariff-inclusive domestic price.
•
At that higher price, read domestic quantity demanded and quantity supplied; imports equal their difference.
•
Tariff revenue equals the tariff per unit multiplied by the number of units imported.
•
Look for lower consumer surplus, higher producer surplus, government revenue, and deadweight loss from
overproduction and underconsumption.
WATCH OUT
Do not treat tariff revenue as full welfare loss. Part of the consumer loss is transferred to producers and
government; the production and consumption distortions are net losses.
WORKED EXAMPLE: FINDING TARIFF REVENUE
At the $4 world price, domestic quantity demanded is
250 thousand and domestic quantity supplied is 50
thousand, so free-trade imports are 200 thousand. A
$2 tariff raises the domestic price to $6; quantity
demanded falls to 200 thousand and quantity
supplied rises to 100 thousand. Imports fall to 100
thousand, and government tariff revenue is $2 x 100
thousand = $200 thousand.
