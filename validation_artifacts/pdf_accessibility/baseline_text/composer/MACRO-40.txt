Disinflation and Policy
THE CORE IDEA
Disinflation lowers the inflation rate but usually creates temporary output and employment costs while
expectations adjust. Contraction reduces aggregate demand and inflation pressure, normally creating a
short-run output gap and higher unemployment. Expected inflation adjusts, the SRPC shifts, and
unemployment returns toward its natural rate. Disinflation is a decline in the inflation rate; deflation is a
decline in the price level.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Disinflation means the inflation rate is falling, not that the price level is falling.
•
Contractionary policy initially moves the economy along the existing short-run Phillips curve toward higher
unemployment.
•
As expected inflation falls, the short-run Phillips curve shifts downward.
•
Credible policy and flexible expectations can reduce the output and employment cost of lowering inflation.
WATCH OUT
Do not confuse a lower positive inflation rate with a falling price level. Disinflation is a decline in the
inflation rate; deflation is a decline in the price level.
WORKED EXAMPLE: LOWERING INFLATION
Contractionary policy first moves the economy along
SRPC2 from b to d, lowering inflation from I2 to I1
while unemployment rises from U2 to U3. As
expected inflation adjusts downward, SRPC shifts to
SRPC1 and unemployment returns to U2 at point c.
Disinflation creates a temporary employment cost.
CHECK YOURSELF
Inflation falls from 5% to 2% but remains positive. Did the price level fall?
