Incentives
THE CORE IDEA
Incentives change the expected benefits or costs of a choice, so they can alter behavior at the margin. The
fee alters the next trip's marginal cost, which can change the decision. With limited time, the higher wage
makes study more costly in forgone earnings. People respond to incentives, but they may respond in ways
that improve the metric while hurting the real goal. The toll raises the cost of using the road, which gives
drivers an incentive to change behavior.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
A speed bonus makes workers finish faster, but quality falls.
•
A new toll reduces traffic on a busy road.
•
A toll is added to a crowded bridge, and some drivers switch routes.
•
A school rewards high test pass rates.
•
An incentive changes the payoff to a choice; it need not be monetary or guarantee a result.
WATCH OUT
A common mistake is to define incentives as cash payments or guaranteed behavior. An incentive
changes the expected payoff to a choice; it may be monetary or nonmonetary, and people can respond
differently.
WORKED EXAMPLE: RESPONDING TO INCENTIVES
A city charges a $4 congestion fee for downtown trips during rush hour. A driver whose next trip
provides only $3 of benefit will now avoid that trip because its marginal cost has risen by $4. A
commuter whose trip is worth $10 will still travel. The fee changes behavior at the margin without
guaranteeing that every driver responds in the same way.
