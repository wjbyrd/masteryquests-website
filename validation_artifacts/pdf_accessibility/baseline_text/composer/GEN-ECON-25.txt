International Trade: Quotas
THE CORE IDEA
An import quota directly limits the quantity of a good that may enter the country. A binding quota reduces
imports below their free-trade level, raises the domestic price, increases domestic production, and reduces
domestic consumption. The price gap between the domestic quota price and the world price creates quota
rent. Who receives that rent affects the country's national-surplus result, while the production and
consumption distortions create deadweight loss.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Begin with the free-trade outcome at the world price.
•
A binding import quota limits imports below their free-trade quantity.
•
The restriction raises domestic price, increases domestic production, and reduces domestic consumption.
•
The gap between the quota price and world price creates quota rent on each permitted import.
•
Look for deadweight loss from excessive domestic production and reduced consumption.
WATCH OUT
Do not assume quota rent automatically becomes government revenue. It accrues to whoever holds the
import rights; auctioning those rights can transfer the rent to government. The recipient matters for
national welfare, while production and consumption distortions create deadweight loss.
WORKED EXAMPLE: QUOTA PRICE AND IMPORTS
Before trade, domestic supply and demand intersect at a price of
$7. With free trade, buyers can purchase at the $3 world price,
which creates imports. The quota limits imports, so the domestic
price rises to $6. The difference between the $6 quota price and
the $3 world price is $3 per imported unit; that $3 gap is quota
rent.
