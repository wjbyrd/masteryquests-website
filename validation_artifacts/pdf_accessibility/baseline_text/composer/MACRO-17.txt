Natural Rate of Unemployment
THE CORE IDEA
The natural rate of unemployment is the unemployment that remains when the economy is operating near its
normal long-run level. It includes frictional unemployment from ordinary job search and structural unemployment
from mismatches between workers and available jobs. It excludes cyclical unemployment caused by recessions or
unusually strong expansions. Actual unemployment can move above or below the natural rate over the business
cycle. Cyclical unemployment equals actual unemployment minus the natural rate. The natural rate can also
change as demographics, labor-market institutions, matching technology, or persistent skill mismatches change.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Frictional and structural unemployment are combined into a long-run benchmark.
•
A recession pushes actual unemployment above the benchmark.
•
An unusually tight labor market pushes actual unemployment below the benchmark.
•
Job matching, worker skills, demographics, or institutions change over time.
•
The problem asks for the gap between actual and natural unemployment.
WATCH OUT
The natural rate is neither zero nor permanently fixed. Workers still search for jobs and industries still
change in a healthy economy. A temporary recession raises cyclical unemployment, not automatically
the natural rate; however, lasting skill loss or labor-force detachment can eventually raise structural
unemployment and the natural rate.
WORKED EXAMPLE: FINDING CYCLICAL UNEMPLOYMENT
Suppose actual unemployment is 7% and the natural rate is 5%. Cyclical unemployment = 7% - 5% = 2
percentage points. The positive gap indicates recession-related unemployment on top of normal
frictional and structural unemployment. If demand recovers and the natural rate remains 5%, actual
unemployment should move back toward 5%. It need not fall to zero because normal job search and
skill mismatch remain.
CHECK YOURSELF
A job-matching platform shortens job searches, but a major industry shift leaves many workers without
the skills employers need. How could each change affect the natural rate?
