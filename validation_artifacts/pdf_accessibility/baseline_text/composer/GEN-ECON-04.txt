Marginal Analysis
THE CORE IDEA
Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit of the next unit or small change with its additional cost. A
decision should expand while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost and stop before the next unit costs
more than it adds. Sunk costs do not affect this forward-looking comparison, while costs or benefits imposed
on others matter in social marginal analysis.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Look for a decision about one more unit, one more hour, or a small change in an activity.
•
Compare the additional benefit of that change with its additional cost.
•
Continue while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost; stop before the next unit costs more than it adds.
•
Ignore totals or averages when the question asks whether the next unit is worthwhile.
WATCH OUT
A common mistake is to decide from total benefit or total cost. Marginal analysis compares the
additional benefit and additional cost of the next unit.
WORKED EXAMPLE: COMPARING MARGINAL BENEFIT AND COST
A warehouse earns $15 from handling one additional box, and the marginal cost of that box is $13.
Marginal net benefit is $15 - $13 = $2, so handling the box increases profit. If the next box cost $17
instead, its marginal net benefit would be -$2 and the warehouse should stop before that box.
CHECK YOURSELF
Total cost is $100 for 10 units, and producing an eleventh unit adds $15. Which number is average cost,
and which is marginal cost?
