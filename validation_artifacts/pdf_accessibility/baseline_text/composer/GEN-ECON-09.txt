Comparative Advantage and Gains from Trade
THE CORE IDEA
Comparative advantage belongs to the producer with the lower opportunity cost, even when another
producer has an absolute advantage in both goods. Compute the forgone alternative per unit, then select the
lower opportunity cost. Specialization creates potential output gains; the trading terms determine whether
both sides gain. Use opportunity cost and net benefits to evaluate specialization and exchange. Different
opportunity costs create room for specialization and mutually beneficial exchange.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
Use opportunity cost and net benefits to evaluate specialization and exchange.
•
Different opportunity costs create room for specialization and mutually beneficial exchange.
•
One lamp costs Ari 2 rugs and costs Bea 6 rugs.
•
One producer is more productive in both goods.
•
Two firms specialize and create 40 extra units of combined output.
WATCH OUT
A common mistake is to choose the producer with the greatest absolute output. Comparative advantage
depends on lower opportunity cost, and a mutually beneficial terms-of-trade ratio lies between the two
opportunity costs.
WORKED EXAMPLE: FINDING COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE
Ari can produce either 12 lamps or 24 rugs, so one lamp costs Ari 24/12 = 2 rugs. Bea can produce
either 10 lamps or 60 rugs, so one lamp costs Bea 60/10 = 6 rugs. Ari has comparative advantage in
lamps because 2 rugs is the lower opportunity cost; Bea has comparative advantage in rugs because
one rug costs Bea 10/60 = 1/6 lamp, below Ari's 12/24 = 1/2 lamp. Specialization according to those
lower opportunity costs can increase combined output.
