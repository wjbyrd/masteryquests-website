THE CORE IDEA
The Federal Reserve influences bank reserves, the money supply, and short-term interest rates through
several policy tools. Open-market purchases add reserves to the banking system, while open-market sales
remove them. Administered rates change the incentives banks face when borrowing or holding reserves.
Changes in reserves can affect bank lending and deposit creation, although the actual expansion of money
may be smaller than the simple textbook multiplier predicts.
HOW TO RECOGNIZE IT
•
An open-market purchase adds reserves; in the limited-reserves model this tends to lower short-term rates.
•
An open-market sale removes reserves; in the limited-reserves model this tends to raise short-term rates.
•
Lower administered rates ease monetary conditions; higher administered rates restrain them.
•
In an ample-reserves system, administered rates are the main tool for controlling short-term rates.
WATCH OUT
Do not assume every tool in a policy package pushes bank lending in the same direction. The
open-market purchase is expansionary, while a higher return on reserves can weaken the lending
response.
WORKED EXAMPLE: OPEN-MARKET PURCHASE AND DEPOSIT EXPANSION
The central bank buys $50 million of securities, adding $50 million to bank reserves. With a 20%
required reserve ratio, no excess reserves, and full redepositing, the simple deposit multiplier is 1 / 0.20
= 5. Maximum deposit expansion is $50 million x 5 = $250 million. Currency drain or additional reserve
holding makes the result smaller; it cannot be calculated by multiplying 5 by the lending share.
