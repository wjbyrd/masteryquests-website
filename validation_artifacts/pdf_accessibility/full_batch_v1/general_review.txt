GEN-ECON-01 Scarcity [original]
CORE: Scarcity means people, businesses, and governments have limited resources but unlimited wants. Because resources such as time, money, labor, and raw materials are limited, choices must be made. Every choice involves a tradeoff, and those tradeoffs force individuals and societies to decide how to use scarce resources. Scarcity is the basic problem that economics studies.
RECOGNITION: Resources are limited, but wants are greater than what can be produced. | A choice must be made between competing uses. | Getting more of one thing means giving up something else. | The question focuses on limits, tradeoffs, or unmet wants.
WATCH: Scarcity does not mean there is almost none of a good. It means the good is limited relative to wants. Even time and money are scarce because they have alternative uses.
WORKED: A student has two hours before an exam and must choose between studying economics or finishing a history paper. The student cannot fully do both. This is scarcity because time is limited, so choosing one use means giving up the other.

GEN-ECON-02 Incentives [original]
CORE: Incentives change the expected benefits or costs of a choice, so they can alter behavior at the margin. The fee alters the next trip's marginal cost, which can change the decision. With limited time, the higher wage makes study more costly in forgone earnings. People respond to incentives, but they may respond in ways that improve the metric while hurting the real goal. The toll raises the cost of using the road, which gives drivers an incentive to change behavior.
RECOGNITION: A speed bonus makes workers finish faster, but quality falls. | A new toll reduces traffic on a busy road. | A toll is added to a crowded bridge, and some drivers switch routes. | A school rewards high test pass rates. | An incentive changes the payoff to a choice; it need not be monetary or guarantee a result.
WATCH: A common mistake is to define incentives as cash payments or guaranteed behavior. An incentive changes the expected payoff to a choice; it may be monetary or nonmonetary, and people can respond differently.
WORKED: A city charges a $4 congestion fee for downtown trips during rush hour. A driver whose next trip provides only $3 of benefit will now avoid that trip because its marginal cost has risen by $4. A commuter whose trip is worth $10 will still travel. The fee changes behavior at the margin without guaranteeing that every driver responds in the same way.

GEN-ECON-03 Opportunity Cost [original]
CORE: Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up when a choice is made. Scarcity makes opportunity cost unavoidable because using time, money, or resources for one purpose prevents their use elsewhere. The cost is the single best forgone option, valued from the decision maker's perspective, and it can be monetary or nonmonetary.
RECOGNITION: Look for a choice between two or more alternatives under a time, money, or resource constraint. | Identify the next-best alternative that the decision maker gives up. | Value the forgone alternative from the decision maker's perspective. | Do not add every rejected option; opportunity cost is the single best alternative forgone.
WATCH: A common mistake is to add the value of every forgone option. Opportunity cost is the value of the single next-best alternative, not the sum of all alternatives.
WORKED: A student can spend Saturday working for $80, studying for an exam worth an estimated $60 benefit, or attending a concert valued at $40. If the student studies, the best forgone alternative is the $80 work shift. The opportunity cost of studying is therefore $80, not $120 and not the concert's $40 value.

GEN-ECON-04 Marginal Analysis [original]
CORE: Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit of the next unit or small change with its additional cost. A decision should expand while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost and stop before the next unit costs more than it adds. Sunk costs do not affect this forward-looking comparison, while costs or benefits imposed on others matter in social marginal analysis.
RECOGNITION: Look for a decision about one more unit, one more hour, or a small change in an activity. | Compare the additional benefit of that change with its additional cost. | Continue while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost; stop before the next unit costs more than it adds. | Ignore totals or averages when the question asks whether the next unit is worthwhile.
WATCH: A common mistake is to decide from total benefit or total cost. Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit and additional cost of the next unit.
WORKED: A warehouse earns $15 from handling one additional box, and the marginal cost of that box is $13. Marginal net benefit is $15 - $13 = $2, so handling the box increases profit. If the next box cost $17 instead, its marginal net benefit would be -$2 and the warehouse should stop before that box.

GEN-ECON-05 Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics [original]
CORE: Microeconomics studies choices by individual households, firms, workers, and markets, while macroeconomics studies economy-wide outcomes such as output, inflation, and unemployment. One event can require both market-level and economy-wide analysis. Macro conclusions require appropriate aggregation across people, firms, and markets. Macroeconomics examines economy-wide aggregates such as output, inflation, and unemployment. A single firm's hiring decision is microeconomics.
RECOGNITION: A report tracks national inflation, unemployment, and total output. | A firm decides how much labor to hire at one restaurant. | Microeconomics can use massive data while focusing on individual choices and specific markets. | A bakery studies how customers respond to a muffin price increase. | Microeconomics studies choices by households, firms, and specific markets.
WATCH: A common mistake is to label any large organization as macroeconomic. The classification depends on the unit of analysis: one decision-maker is micro; economy-wide aggregates are macro.
WORKED: A restaurant deciding whether to hire one more server is a microeconomic question because it concerns one firm's choice. The national unemployment rate is a macroeconomic outcome because it aggregates labor-market conditions across the economy. The subject's size is not decisive; the unit of analysis is.

GEN-ECON-06 Positive vs. Normative Analysis [original]
CORE: Positive analysis makes claims that evidence can test, while normative analysis applies value judgments about what should be done. Identify what observation could support or contradict a testable claim. A positive claim can be false. A normative claim ranks goals or outcomes using values; evidence can inform that judgment but cannot settle the values alone.
RECOGNITION: A transit study predicts that a lower fare will increase ridership. | Ridership data can test the prediction even if it turns out to be wrong. | A council member says parks should receive more funding because access is fair. | A commissioner says the city ought to favor safer streets even if travel takes longer.
WATCH: Do not treat positive as good, true, or politically neutral. A positive claim is one that evidence could in principle test, even if the claim is false.
WORKED: The statement 'A lower bus fare will increase ridership' is positive because ridership data can support or contradict it. The statement 'The city should lower bus fares because access is fair' is normative because it applies a value judgment. Evidence can inform both statements, but only the first is directly testable as written.

GEN-ECON-07 Models and Assumptions [original]
CORE: Economic models simplify reality to isolate the relationships that matter for a particular question. The two-good abstraction exposes scarcity and opportunity cost without describing every output. Evaluate a model by its purpose, assumptions, performance, and relevant context. Extra complexity is useful only when its benefits exceed lost clarity or robustness. Apply a model where its important assumptions remain reasonable.

WATCH: Do not use the model without checking its purpose, assumptions, or evidence. Evaluate a model by its purpose, assumptions, performance, and relevant context.

GEN-ECON-08 Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) [original]
CORE: A production possibilities frontier shows the combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its current resources and technology. A bowed-out PPF shows increasing opportunity cost as resources are shifted toward one good. Opportunity cost rises because resources are specialized and become less well suited as they are shifted into the other good. A PPF shows possible production combinations given current resources and technology.
RECOGNITION: A PPF shows possible production combinations given current resources and technology. | A new technology improves only medical production. | An economy first removes unemployment, then gains new machinery. | Using idle resources changes the production point; resources or technology shift the frontier.
WATCH: Do not confuse a PPF with a market curve. A PPF shows possible production combinations given current resources and technology. Using idle resources changes the production point; resources or technology shift the frontier.
WORKED: On the PPF, moving from point C (15 units of X and 35 of Y) to point D (21 of X and 20 of Y) gains 6 units of X but gives up 15 units of Y. The total opportunity cost is 15 units of Y, or 15/6 = 2.5 units of Y per additional unit of X.

GEN-ECON-09 Comparative Advantage and Gains from Trade [original]
CORE: Comparative advantage belongs to the producer with the lower opportunity cost, even when another producer has an absolute advantage in both goods. Compute the forgone alternative per unit, then select the lower opportunity cost. Specialization creates potential output gains; the trading terms determine whether both sides gain. Use opportunity cost and net benefits to evaluate specialization and exchange. Different opportunity costs create room for specialization and mutually beneficial exchange.
RECOGNITION: Use opportunity cost and net benefits to evaluate specialization and exchange. | Different opportunity costs create room for specialization and mutually beneficial exchange. | One lamp costs Ari 2 rugs and costs Bea 6 rugs. | One producer is more productive in both goods. | Two firms specialize and create 40 extra units of combined output.
WATCH: A common mistake is to choose the producer with the greatest absolute output. Comparative advantage depends on lower opportunity cost, and a mutually beneficial terms-of-trade ratio lies between the two opportunity costs.
WORKED: Ari can produce either 12 lamps or 24 rugs, so one lamp costs Ari 24/12 = 2 rugs. Bea can produce either 10 lamps or 60 rugs, so one lamp costs Bea 60/10 = 6 rugs. Ari has comparative advantage in lamps because 2 rugs is the lower opportunity cost; Bea has comparative advantage in rugs because one rug costs Bea 10/60 = 1/6 lamp, below Ari's 12/24 = 1/2 lamp. Specialization according to those lower opportunity costs can increase combined output.

GEN-ECON-10 Economists and Policy [original]
CORE: Economists use evidence and models to estimate policy effects, identify tradeoffs, and explain uncertainty. Responsible advice reports assumptions, uncertainty, and plausible unintended consequences. Economic analysis clarifies tradeoffs but does not uniquely rank every social goal. Economists can estimate consequences; recommendations also require value judgments. Economists analyze incentives, evidence, and tradeoffs; elected officials make policy choices.
RECOGNITION: An economist estimates a policy’s effects, then recommends adoption. | Economists analyze incentives, evidence, and tradeoffs; elected officials make policy choices. | Policy evaluation requires explicit objectives and evidence about consequences. | A policy model predicts large benefits, but the data come from a different population and period. | Good advice makes assumptions, tradeoffs, and uncertainty visible.
WATCH: A common mistake is to give economists authority to choose society's values. Economists can estimate consequences and tradeoffs; the policy choice also requires normative judgments.
WORKED: An economist estimates that a proposed transit subsidy will increase ridership by 8%, but the estimate comes from a city with denser development. Professional advice reports the predicted benefit, explains the uncertain transfer to the local setting, and identifies fiscal costs. Whether those consequences justify the policy also depends on public values and priorities.

GEN-ECON-11 Competitive Markets [original]
CORE: A competitive market contains many independent buyers and sellers, usually trading products that buyers view as close substitutes. No single participant controls enough of the market to set the market price. A small firm is therefore a price taker: it chooses how much to sell at the price created by market supply and demand. Competition becomes stronger when firms can enter or exit, buyers can compare offers, and switching among sellers is easy.
RECOGNITION: Many independent firms sell the same product or close substitutes. | One seller supplies only a small share of total market output. | A firm accepts a price determined by overall market supply and demand. | Buyers can compare prices and switch sellers without much difficulty. | Entry by new firms or exit by existing firms changes competitive pressure.
WATCH: A market is not strictly competitive just because it has several sellers. Product differentiation, search costs, network effects, or barriers to entry can still give firms pricing power. The key question is whether one seller can raise its price persistently without losing many customers to available alternatives.
WORKED: A wheat farmer is one of thousands selling a standardized crop. The posted market price is $6 per bushel. If this farmer asks $7 while other farmers still charge $6, buyers can purchase identical wheat elsewhere. The farmer is too small to move the market price, so raising the price would sharply reduce sales. The evidence - many sellers, a standardized product, and easy switching - identifies price-taking behavior.

GEN-ECON-12 Demand & the Law of Demand [original]
CORE: Demand describes the quantities buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices, holding other demand conditions constant. Quantity demanded is the amount buyers choose at one particular price. The law of demand states that a lower own price increases quantity demanded and a higher own price decreases it, all else equal. An own-price change causes movement between points on the same demand curve; it does not shift the curve.
RECOGNITION: The good's own price changes while income, tastes, expectations, related-good prices, and the number of buyers remain constant. | The question asks for quantity demanded at a particular price. | Two points lie on the same downward-sloping demand curve. | A lower price is paired with a larger quantity demanded.
WATCH: A common mistake is to call every change in quantity a shift in demand. If the good's own price changes and buyers move from one point to another on the same demand curve, quantity demanded changes. Demand itself shifts only when a nonprice determinant changes.
WORKED: On the gourmet-donut graph, point A shows a price of $10 and quantity demanded of 100 thousand. Point B shows a lower price of $5 and quantity demanded of 225 thousand. Because both points lie on the same demand curve and only the good's own price changes, the move from A to B is an increase in quantity demanded of 125 thousand, not an increase in demand.
CHECK: The price of a concert ticket falls and buyers move to another point on the same demand curve. What changes: demand or quantity demanded?

GEN-ECON-13 Determinants of Demand [original]
CORE: Demand shifts when a nonprice determinant changes. Demand increases and shifts right when buyers want more at every price; demand decreases and shifts left when they want less at every price. Important determinants include income, tastes and preferences, expectations, the number of buyers, and the prices of substitutes and complements. The direction of an income effect depends on whether the good is normal or inferior, and the effect of a related-good price depends on whether the goods are substitutes or complements.
RECOGNITION: Consumer income, preferences, expectations, or the number of buyers changes. | The price of a substitute or complement changes. | Quantity demanded changes at the same price. | The entire demand curve moves left or right rather than moving to another point on one curve.
WATCH: Do not treat a change in the good's own price as a demand shifter. Own-price changes create movements along the existing curve. A nonprice determinant changes how much buyers want at every price and shifts the entire curve.
WORKED: At a coffee price of $10, the original curve D0 shows quantity demanded of 50 thousand, while D1 shows 125 thousand. The price of coffee is held constant, so the 75-thousand increase cannot be a movement along D0. It is a rightward shift from D0 to D1, meaning a nonprice determinant has increased demand.

GEN-ECON-16 Market Equilibrium [original]
CORE: Market equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At that price, buyers can purchase the amount they want and sellers can sell the amount they offer, so there is no pressure for price to change. A demand or supply shift creates a new intersection and can change both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.

GEN-ECON-23 International Trade: World Prices [original]
CORE: A world price lets a small country trade at a price determined outside its domestic market. When the world price is below the domestic no-trade equilibrium price, domestic buyers demand more than domestic firms supply and the country imports the difference. When the world price is above the no-trade price, domestic firms supply more than buyers demand and the country exports the difference. Trade changes domestic production, consumption, and the distribution of surplus.

GEN-ECON-24 International Trade: Tariffs [original]
CORE: A tariff is a tax on imported goods that raises the domestic price above the world price. Domestic firms respond by producing more, consumers buy less, and imports fall. Consumers lose surplus, domestic producers gain surplus, and the government collects tariff revenue. The tariff also prevents mutually beneficial trades, creating deadweight loss from excessive domestic production and reduced consumption.
RECOGNITION: Begin with an importing country whose world price is below the domestic no-trade price. | Add the tariff per unit to the world price to find the tariff-inclusive domestic price. | At that higher price, read domestic quantity demanded and quantity supplied; imports equal their difference. | Tariff revenue equals the tariff per unit multiplied by the number of units imported. | Look for lower consumer surplus, higher producer surplus, government revenue, and deadweight loss from overproduction and underconsumption.
WATCH: Do not treat tariff revenue as full welfare loss. Part of the consumer loss is transferred to producers and government; the production and consumption distortions are net losses.
WORKED: At the $4 world price, domestic quantity demanded is 250 thousand and domestic quantity supplied is 50 thousand, so free-trade imports are 200 thousand. A $2 tariff raises the domestic price to $6; quantity demanded falls to 200 thousand and quantity supplied rises to 100 thousand. Imports fall to 100 thousand, and government tariff revenue is $2 x 100 thousand = $200 thousand.

GEN-ECON-25 International Trade: Quotas [original]
CORE: An import quota directly limits the quantity of a good that may enter the country. A binding quota reduces imports below their free-trade level, raises the domestic price, increases domestic production, and reduces domestic consumption. The price gap between the domestic quota price and the world price creates quota rent. Who receives that rent affects the country's national-surplus result, while the production and consumption distortions create deadweight loss.
RECOGNITION: Begin with the free-trade outcome at the world price. | A binding import quota limits imports below their free-trade quantity. | The restriction raises domestic price, increases domestic production, and reduces domestic consumption. | The gap between the quota price and world price creates quota rent on each permitted import. | Look for deadweight loss from excessive domestic production and reduced consumption.
WATCH: Do not assume quota rent automatically becomes government revenue. It accrues to whoever holds the import rights; auctioning those rights can transfer the rent to government. The recipient matters for national welfare, while production and consumption distortions create deadweight loss.
WORKED: Before trade, domestic supply and demand intersect at a price of $7. With free trade, buyers can purchase at the $3 world price, which creates imports. The quota limits imports, so the domestic price rises to $6. The difference between the $6 quota price and the $3 world price is $3 per imported unit; that $3 gap is quota rent.

GEN-ECON-26 International Trade: Policy and Distribution [original]
CORE: Trade protection redistributes income and usually reduces total surplus, so a policy claim must be evaluated against its costs and alternatives. Security, infant-industry, employment, and bargaining arguments may identify legitimate goals, but each claim requires evidence that the policy can achieve the goal and that a less costly policy is unavailable. Sound analysis separates measurable economic effects from the value judgments used to choose among them.
RECOGNITION: Security can be legitimate, but the claim still requires evidence and comparison. | Compare the free-trade and quota outcomes. | An infant-industry argument requires a temporary, evidence-based learning mechanism and net benefits. | Efficiency concerns total surplus; equity concerns distribution. | Adjustment goals can be pursued through policies with different efficiency costs.
WATCH: Do not ignore efficiency or distribution evidence. Security can be legitimate, but the claim still requires evidence and comparison. An infant-industry argument requires a temporary, evidence-based learning mechanism and net benefits.
WORKED: Suppose protection preserves 1,000 domestic jobs but costs consumers $30 million each year.
