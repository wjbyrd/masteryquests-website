  1. Setting aside current income for future use (key)

Income is $1,000, net taxes are $180, consumption is $650, and government purchases are $220. If government purchases then rise by $30 with the other values fixed, which result follows?

  1. Private saving stays $170; public and national saving fall $30 (key)
  2. Private saving falls to $140; public saving is unchanged, shifting investment demand left
  3. Private saving stays $170; public and national saving each rise by $30, shifting supply right

Feedback: Private saving is Y − T − C = $170. Higher G does not change that amount, but it lowers T − G and national saving by $30, so loanable-funds supply shifts left.

In a closed economy, private saving rises by $20 billion while public saving falls by $35 billion. At the same time, new technology raises desired investment. What can be concluded without shift magnitudes?

  1. Supply shifts left, demand shifts right; the rate rises and quantity is ambiguous (key)
  2. Both curves shift right; quantity rises, while the real rate is ambiguous
  3. Supply shifts left only; both the real rate and quantity must fall

Feedback: National saving falls by $15 billion, shifting supply left. Technology shifts investment demand right. Both changes raise the real rate, but they push equilibrium quantity in opposite directions.

A household buys existing shares from another investor. Later, the corporation issues new bonds and uses the proceeds to build a factory. Which statement correctly distinguishes the transactions?

  1. Only the new factory is current GDP investment (key)
  2. Both securities trades are current GDP investment because money changes hands
  3. Only the existing-share purchase is current GDP investment because it changes ownership

Feedback: Existing-share trades transfer financial claims. Newly issued bonds can channel saving to the firm, but the newly produced factory is the transaction counted as current macroeconomic investment.
