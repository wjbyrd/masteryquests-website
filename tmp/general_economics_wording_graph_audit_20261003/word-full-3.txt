P73-MARG-L-006 | A treatment slot yields 88 benefit points and uses supplies costing 42 points. The staff time would otherwise support readiness worth 35 points. A method cuts supply cost by 8 but reduces the treatment benefit by 12. Which version is preferable to leaving staff in readiness?
KEY The original slot, with an 11-point net gain
    The cheaper method, with a 15-point net gain
    Neither, because readiness must be added to benefit
    Both versions give the same gain because both save supplies
FEEDBACK Original net gain is 88 - 42 - 35 = 11. The cheaper method gives 76 - 34 - 35 = 7. Both beat the alternative, but cost saving alone does not make the revised method better.

P77-PVN-EL-023 | Two analysts agree that an archive plan preserves more records but delays public access. One recommends it; the other rejects it. What additional finding would show a factual disagreement as well as a value disagreement?
    They agree on the delay but differ on whether it is acceptable
    They use the same probabilities but prefer different distributions of access

    They give different ethical priority to immediate public access

P71-SCAR-H-004 | A delivery disruption leaves a hospital temporarily short of oxygen cylinders, while its limited budget also restricts how many backup systems it can maintain. Which statement is accurate?
KEY The disruption is a shortage; the budget tradeoff reflects ongoing scarcity
    Both conditions are temporary shortages because money is involved
    Both conditions are scarcity only if oxygen receives a market price
    The backup budget eliminates scarcity once deliveries resume
FEEDBACK The disruption leaves fewer cylinders available than the hospital needs under current conditions. The budget constraint requires choices among backup systems and other uses of funds, illustrating scarcity. This particular disruption is temporary; shortages need not be temporary, and scarcity is not limited to the long run.

ECON-MG-ELITE-318 | A market moves from $40 and 500 units before a tax to a buyer price of $46, seller receipt of $34, and 420 units afterward. What is the seller-burden rectangle on units still traded, excluding the surplus lost on missing trades?
KEY $2,520
    $3,000
    $5,040
    $14,280
FEEDBACK The seller loses $40 − $34 = $6 per continuing unit, so the rectangle is $6 × 420 = $2,520. Using 500 applies the burden to trades that no longer occur. The $12 wedge times 420 is total revenue, not the seller share alone.

ECON-MG-HARD-279 | Refer to the tax incidence graph. A seller group divides the fall in total net-of-tax sales receipts by the 80 units still sold and calls the result its tax burden per continuing unit. Why is that calculation misleading?
KEY The $540 receipt decline includes fewer units sold; the price burden on each continuing unit is $3.
    The $540 receipt decline is entirely a price effect; the burden on each continuing unit is $6.75.
    The $240 receipt decline includes fewer units sold; the price burden on each continuing unit is $6.
    The $300 receipt decline is entirely a quantity effect; the price burden on each continuing unit is $0.
FEEDBACK Receipts fall from $15 x 100 = $1,500 to $12 x 80 = $960, a $540 decline. This combines $300 from 20 fewer units valued at the old price and $240 from receiving $3 less on each of 80 continuing sales. The per-unit seller tax burden is $15 - $12 = $3; gross receipts are not a measure of producer surplus.
