elasticity_business_pricing
A product initially has few close substitutes. A 10% price increase reduces its quantity demanded by 4%. Later, several close substitutes become available; the same 10% price increase reduces quantity demanded by 16%. The comparison uses the same adjustment period, and income, tastes and the product’s necessity status are unchanged. A manager claims the larger response is merely a movement along demand and provides no evidence that responsiveness changed. Which evaluation is strongest?
A:Accept the claim: the ratios 0.4 and 1.6 describe movements along demand. Because the product itself is unchanged, responsiveness should be treated as constant despite the difference between those ratios.
B:Reject the claim: elasticity rises from 0.4 to 1.6 as close substitutes expand. Each price experiment moves along its own demand curve, while the comparison shows changed responsiveness across periods. [KEY]
C:Reject the claim: elasticity rises from 0.4 to 1.6 because consumers have more time to adjust later. Equal experiment lengths do not constrain the time-horizon explanation for the larger response.
D:Reject the claim: elasticity rises from 0.4 to 1.6 because the product becomes more like a luxury. A larger observed response establishes changed necessity status despite the stated comparison controls.
Feedback: Using the stated percentage responses, |Ed| changes from 4/10=0.4 to 16/10=1.6. Each price change causes movement along the demand curve applicable then. The different responsiveness across periods is consistent with the new substitutes; equal adjustment periods and unchanged necessity status rule out the other explanations.
