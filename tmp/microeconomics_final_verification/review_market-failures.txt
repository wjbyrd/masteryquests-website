A crop price rises after a drought reduces harvests. A separate processing plant also discharges unpriced waste into a river. Which diagnosis and targeted response distinguish scarcity from market failure?
A:The crop price can signal genuine scarcity; a policy aimed at the waste should target its external cost rather than treating the high price alone as proof of failure. [KEY]
B:Both are market failures solely because prices and waste increased, so a price ceiling necessarily corrects both.
C:Neither can be market failure because any outcome with a market price is efficient.
D:The drought is asymmetric information and the waste is merely a transfer between buyers and sellers.
Feedback: A supply loss can raise a competitive price without creating a distortion. Unpriced harm omits social cost from the private decision and calls for a response to that specific wedge.

Buyers cannot observe product risk before purchase. Mandatory disclosure accurately reports it, but only in a format most buyers cannot interpret. A simplified tested label helps buyers compare risks at lower reading cost. Which comparison is defensible?
A:Accurate disclosure necessarily eliminates the problem regardless of comprehension, so testing the label adds no information.
B:Both address an information problem, but the tested label can improve usable information more effectively; accuracy alone does not establish that the first format changes decisions. [KEY]
C:The unreadable format solves an externality while the label creates monopoly power.
D:Neither addresses market failure because information costs cannot affect mutually beneficial trades.
Feedback: The original problem is an information gap about risk. Disclosure must be usable to affect choices; evidence about comprehension and behavior distinguishes formal disclosure from an effective remedy.

A corrective policy would prevent $120 of external harm while using $35 of compliance resources and $100 of administrative resources. A simpler policy prevents $80 of harm and uses $20 in total resources. Which recommendation follows from these estimates alone?
A:The full policy is preferable because it prevents more harm, regardless of implementation cost.
B:Both policies are equally desirable because each reduces a market failure.
C:The full policy has net benefit −$15 and the simpler policy +$60; the simpler remedy is preferable to both the full policy and doing nothing. [KEY]
D:Doing nothing is preferable because any administrative cost makes intervention inefficient.
Feedback: Net benefits are 120−35−100=−15 and 80−20=60. The scale of the original failure does not make every proposed correction worthwhile.

A disclosure rule is forecast to prevent $90 of harm at $20 resource cost. A pilot finds that confusion causes $35 of additional harm and that only $50 of the original harm is actually prevented. A simpler label prevents $40 at $10 cost with no added confusion. Which comparison uses the observed performance?
A:The original rule has +$70 net benefit because forecasts should replace contradictory pilot evidence.
B:The original rule has +$30 net benefit because confusion is only a transfer, making it equivalent to the simpler label.
C:The original rule has net benefit −$5, while the simpler label has +$30; revise the policy despite its initially plausible mechanism. [KEY]
D:Neither can be compared because any information rule guarantees an efficient outcome.
Feedback: Use the pilot’s actual effects: 50−35−20=−5. The alternative yields 40−10=30. The intended mechanism is relevant, but implementation evidence determines the net result.

A market maximizes measured total surplus but distributes it unequally; no externality, information gap, market power or public-good problem is identified. Policy A transfers $30 between participants without resource cost or changed behavior. Policy B makes the same transfer but destroys $8 of gains through changed allocation. Which assessment is correct?
A:Inequality motivates a separate equity judgment; A preserves measured surplus while B trades $8 of it for the same intended redistribution. [KEY]
B:Both policies correct a demonstrated efficiency failure because any unequal outcome is inefficient.
C:B must be more efficient because changing allocation proves it addresses a market failure.
D:A raises total surplus by $30 while B raises it by $22 because transfers are new resources.
Feedback: An unequal split does not by itself demonstrate a missing efficiency condition. The costless transfer changes distribution; B additionally sacrifices $8 of gains. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable requires an equity judgment.
